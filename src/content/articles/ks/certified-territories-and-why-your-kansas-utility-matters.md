In most conversations about land, who sends the electric bill is a footnote. In
Kansas it is closer to a headline, for a reason that is specific to how this
state is organized.

## Kansas assigns service areas

Electric service in Kansas is carved into certified territories. The utility
holding the territory serves it exclusively, and a customer inside it does not
select an alternative. The Kansas Corporation Commission maintains the map.

That structure exists for sensible reasons and it is not unusual by itself. What
makes it matter here is what the legislature attached to it.

## The incentive is tied to the local provider

The Kansas data center sales tax exemption is not automatic. An operator has to
meet several conditions, and one of them is a commitment to purchase electricity
for ten years from the utility certified for the territory where the facility
sits.

Read that again with your own land in mind. Whichever provider holds your ground
is the provider a data center would be locked into for a decade in order to
qualify. That is not a preference a developer can negotiate around by shopping
elsewhere.

The practical consequence is that your provider, and that provider's capacity
and appetite for a very large new load, becomes part of whether a project on
your land is viable at all.

## Who the players are

Evergy serves much of eastern and south central Kansas, including Topeka,
Wichita, Lawrence, and the Kansas side of the Kansas City area.

Across rural Kansas, most ground is served by a local distribution cooperative.
Those cooperatives buy wholesale power through one of two generation and
transmission organizations. Sunflower Electric Power Corporation supplies
members across central and western Kansas. Kansas Electric Power Cooperative
supplies distribution cooperatives across much of the eastern two thirds.

Midwest Energy covers a large part of north central and northwest Kansas and is
unusual in providing both electricity and natural gas.

Kansas also has an unusually large number of city owned electric systems.

## What you should and should not do

Look at your bill. That is the whole task.

Do not call your utility to ask whether they could serve a data center on your
property. Utilities receive a steady stream of speculative questions about large
loads, a landowner asking without a project behind them will not get a useful
answer, and the question has a way of traveling around a small county faster
than you would like.

Do not try to work out line voltages or substation capacity yourself. It is all
public record and checking it is the buyer's job, not yours.

## What is worth noticing

Whether there is a substation near you, and roughly how far. Whether large steel
lattice towers cross your sections, as opposed to the wooden poles that run to
houses and barns. Whether there are turbines in sight, because generation
usually means transmission came with it.

Four sentences describing that is more useful to a buyer than a page of guesses,
and it costs you nothing but a look out the window.
