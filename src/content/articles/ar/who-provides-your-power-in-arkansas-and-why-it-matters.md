When someone asks who sends your electric bill, it can sound like small talk. It
is not. In Arkansas the answer narrows the possibilities considerably before
anyone looks at a map.

## The rough shape of it

Arkansas is served by a handful of large investor owned utilities, a wide
network of rural cooperatives, and a number of city owned systems.

Entergy Arkansas covers much of the central and eastern part of the state.
Southwestern Electric Power Company, usually called SWEPCO, serves a good deal
of the northwest and west. Oklahoma Gas and Electric reaches into western
Arkansas from its Oklahoma system, and Empire District Electric serves a small
area in the north.

Outside those territories, most rural ground is served by a local distribution
cooperative. Those cooperatives buy their wholesale power through Arkansas
Electric Cooperative Corporation, which covers a large share of the state by
area.

A dozen or so Arkansas cities run their own electric utilities, among them
Jonesboro, Conway, North Little Rock, and West Memphis.

## Why any of that changes the answer

A data center is a very large and very steady electrical load. Connecting one is
not a matter of running a bigger service drop. It usually involves high voltage
transmission, a substation with capacity to spare, and a utility willing and able
to commit to serving that load.

Different providers are in different positions on all three. Some have
transmission running through with room on it. Some serve rural territory where
the nearest capable line is a long way off. None of that is a judgment about the
utility, it is just the physical situation.

The practical effect for you is that two farms of identical size and quality can
get completely different answers, purely because of which system they sit on and
what is nearby on that system.

## What you do not need to do

You do not need to call your utility. Please do not, actually, at least not
first. Utilities field a great many speculative inquiries about large loads, and
a landowner asking on their own behalf without a project behind them rarely gets
a useful answer, and occasionally starts a rumor in the county that the
landowner did not intend to start.

You also do not need to know voltages, line ratings, or which substation serves
you. Those are public record and looking them up is the buyer's job.

All you need to do is look at your bill and read the name at the top.

## The one thing worth noticing yourself

Look out the window instead. Tall steel lattice towers marching across the
country carry transmission, and that is the interesting kind. Wooden poles along
the road carry distribution, which is what runs your house and your barn and is
a different thing entirely.

A fenced yard full of insulators, breakers, and equipment is a substation, and
being near one matters a great deal.

If you can describe what you see in those terms, you have given a buyer more
useful information than most landowners manage, and you did it without spending
a dollar or making a single phone call.
